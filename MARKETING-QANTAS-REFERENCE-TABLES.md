## 1.0 — MARKET SEGMENTATION, PRODUCT/SERVICE DIFFERENTIATION AND POSITIONING

### TABLE 1.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition | Detail |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Market segmentation | "Market segmentation occurs when the total market is subdivided into groups of people who share one or more common characteristics." | Increases sales by better understanding and responding to the desires of different target groups • reduces resource waste and the risk of profit loss |
| — Demographic | Dividing by features of the population | Age, sex, income, occupation, cultural background |
| — Geographic | Dividing by geographic location | Urban/rural, region, climate, city size |
| — Psychographic | Dividing by personal characteristics | Attitudes, values, beliefs, lifestyle, motives, opinions |
| — Behavioural | Dividing by the customer's relationship to the product | Loyalty, usage rate, purchase occasion — what the customer does, not who they are |
| Product/service differentiation | "The process of developing and promoting products that are distinguishable from competitors' offerings." | Purpose is a **competitive advantage** • points of difference: customer service, quality, convenience, value for money, environmental and social concerns • gives the business more leverage over price |
| Positioning | "The development of a product image and perception in the mind of the customer relative to competitors." | The marketing mix shapes and maintains the image • positioning sets the foundation for all other marketing strategies, which is why segmentation → differentiation → positioning runs as a sequence |
| Types of positioning | Quality • Price • Competitive • Target-market | Quality = superior features; price = affordability; competitive = countering a rival; target-market = focusing on the most profitable customers |

### TABLE 1.2 — CONNECTIONS

| Connects to | Specific point | Nature of the connection |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **SWOT** | A SWOT identifies which segments the business has the internal strength to serve |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **product life cycle** | Maturity requires segmentation and differentiation to defend share; growth requires expanded distribution |
| **PROCESS** | Market research — **primary and secondary data** | Segmentation is market research applied — surveys, observation and complaint monitoring define the segments |
| **PROCESS** | **Identifying target markets (stage 4)** | The stage segmentation executes: mass vs segmentation vs niche, and which is the primary vs secondary market |
| **PROCESS** | Monitoring & controlling — **market share analysis** | The KPI measuring whether segmentation worked; a lost segment triggers revising the marketing strategy |
| **INFLUENCE** | Customer choice — psychological & sociocultural | Psychographic and behavioural segmentation exist because perception, motives, self-image, social class, family roles and reference groups fragment a market |
| **INFLUENCE** | Customer choice — economic | A downturn moves consumers into the price-sensitive segment |
| **INFLUENCE** | Ethical / consumer laws | Segmentation cannot become discriminatory targeting or illegal price discrimination |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Increasing market share | A dual-brand structure holds the top and bottom of the market simultaneously |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Expanding the product range/mix | Differentiation is what justifies each new line |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Maximising customer service | Segments allow service to be tailored rather than averaged |

### TABLE 1.3 — QANTAS

| # | Sub-point | What Qantas did | Figures |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Competitive positioning | Launched Jetstar as a no-frills counter-brand after losing the budget segment to Virgin | Domestic share fell **80% → 65% after Virgin entered in 2000**; **Jetstar launched May 2004** |
| 2 | Behavioural segmentation (dual brand) | Splits by trip purpose: Jetstar = cost-sensitive leisure, Qantas = premium/corporate, QantasLink = regional | Group carries **60% of Australia's domestic passengers (H1 FY25)**; Jetstar domestic capacity **+8% FY24** (+15% group), leisure earnings **+54% YoY** |
| 3 | Differentiation by cost structure | Moves unprofitable leisure routes to the low-cost brand, keeping the route and the share | **Bangkok and the Gold Coast moved to Jetstar** — retains market share while shedding the cost base |
| 4 | Product/service differentiation | Funds the premium difference in cabins and ground product | **$230M customer experience investment (FY24)**, incl. **$100M refresh of 42 domestic + 9 international lounges**; **A220 cabins** with the largest windows of any single-aisle aircraft |
| 5 | Target-market positioning | Prioritises the corporate segment via Cityflyer, lounges, Qantas Club and the frequent flyer scheme | Holds roughly **80% of the domestic corporate aviation market**; **Cityflyer** (2001) runs Melbourne–Sydney every 30 min; **Sydney–Melbourne is the world's most lucrative route — $1.9bn in H1 FY23** |
| 6 | Quality positioning | Positions as Australia's flagship carrier on safety and reliability | **3.2% cancellation rate in 2023 vs a 3.7% industry average**; ranked **#2 safest airline in the world (AirlineRatings, 2025)** |

**Net effect:** the strategy recovered a 15-point domestic share loss and converted the corporate segment into the highest-revenue route in global aviation.

## 2.0 — PRICE

### TABLE 2.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition | Detail |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Price | "The amount of money a customer is prepared to offer in exchange for a product." | The only element of the marketing mix that generates revenue — the other three cost money |
| **Method:** cost-based | Cost + mark-up determines the price | Cost + (mark-up % × cost) • guarantees costs are covered and a margin is made on each sale |
| **Method:** market-based | Price set according to the level of demand and supply | Higher demand or perceived value = higher price • requires extensive market research |
| **Method:** competition-based | Price set against what competitors charge | Below = undercutting (volume up, margin down, quality perception risk) • equal = matching • above = luxury positioning |
| **Strategy:** price skimming | "Charging the highest possible price for a product with unique features." | Maximises revenue while the product is unique; price falls as competitors catch up |
| **Strategy:** price penetration | "Charging the lowest possible price to capture maximum market share." | Stimulates unsatisfied demand and switches price-sensitive customers from rivals; price can rise once loyalty is established |
| **Strategy:** loss leaders | "Products priced below cost to attract customers." | Relies on the customer buying other full-margin products alongside it |
| **Strategy:** price points | "Setting different prices for similar products (a ladder)." | Widens the customer base across different perceptions of value and income • also signals brand position |
| Price and quality interaction | "The perceived relationship between a product's price and its quality in the mind of the consumer." | High price signals high quality and reinforces a premium position, but only where the experience justifies it; premium pricing during service failure erodes brand equity |

### TABLE 2.2 — CONNECTIONS

| Connects to | Specific point | Nature of the connection |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **product life cycle** | Introduction suits skimming or penetration; maturity forces price competition and cost reduction; decline triggers harvesting |
| **PROCESS** | Market research | Market-based pricing requires demand data; competition-based pricing requires secondary research on rival fares |
| **PROCESS** | **Establishing market objectives** | Price is the lever that most directly moves the revenue and market share objectives, and constrains the load-factor objective |
| **PROCESS** | Implementation — **developing a financial forecast** | Pricing decisions feed into the estimate of expected revenues, costs and profits |
| **PROCESS** | Monitoring — **sales analysis** and **marketing profitability analysis** | The two KPIs measuring a pricing strategy; revising the marketing strategy is where fares move |
| **INFLUENCE** | Economic | Interest rates, inflation and cost-of-living determine willingness and ability to spend; travel is discretionary expenditure, so price is the first lever adjusted in a downturn |
| **INFLUENCE** | Ethical — engaging in fair competition | Competition-based pricing is legal; price fixing/collusion, cartel conduct, resale price maintenance and long-term loss leading are illegal under the CCA 2010 |
| **INFLUENCE** | Consumer laws — price discrimination | Different prices for the same product in separate markets without justification; plus the duty to display the true, all-inclusive price |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Increasing market share | Penetration and loss leaders buy share directly |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Maximising customer service | Price and quality interaction — premium pricing funds the standard that justifies it |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Expanding the product range | Price points create the product range (Economy → Premium Economy → Business → First) |

### TABLE 2.3 — QANTAS

| # | Sub-point | What Qantas did | Figures |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Price penetration | Uses Jetstar to hold the price-sensitive segment through a cost-of-living crisis | **11 million fares under $100 in FY24** (~10M in 2023, alongside 5M reward seats) |
| 2 | Price skimming | Charges a premium on unique, industry-exclusive ultra-long-haul routes | **20% premium on non-stop Perth–London**; Project Sunrise one-way **first class to New York est. $15,000** vs **$6,000 business, $1,500 economy** |
| 3 | Loss leaders | Priced below cost at Jetstar's launch to take initial share | Fares **as low as $19 on Sydney–Melbourne**; **100,000 tickets at $49** plus a two-for-one offer |
| 4 | Price points | Four distinct classes across the ladder | **Sydney–LA business exceeds $8,000; first class reaches $20,000**, against ~**$2,500** for a comparable domestic premium fare |
| 5 | Competition-based pricing | Matches and undercuts new entrants route by route | Rex entered Brisbane–Melbourne: **Virgin matched at $79, Jetstar undercut at $65**; Bonza's 2023 entry drove Qantas' **average revenue per passenger 13.5% lower in 2023 than 2022** |
| 6 | Price and quality interaction | Backs premium prices with premium product, but still tracks the market | **$100M lounge refresh** and **Classic Plus (April 2024, ~$120M)** releasing **20M reward seats**, redemptions **+13%** — while **international yield fell 6.6% (1H FY25)** as capacity normalised |

**Net effect:** the dual-brand structure occupies both ends of the price spectrum at once — $15,000 first-class suites alongside 11 million sub-$100 fares — a range no single-brand competitor holds.

## 3.0 — PROMOTION

### TABLE 3.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition | Detail |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Promotion | "The methods used to inform, persuade and remind a target market about a business' products." | Attracts new customers, heightens awareness, increases sales and increases brand loyalty |
| Advertising | "A paid, non-personal message communicated through a mass medium." | Large reach and controllable, but expensive and one-way • TV, radio, billboards, print, digital, social |
| Personal selling | "The activities of a sales representative directed to a customer in an attempt to make a sale." | Tailored, builds trust, answers specific questions — costly in wages |
| Relationship marketing | "The development of a long-term, cost-effective relationship with individual customers." | Loyalty programs convert one-off buyers into repeat revenue |
| Sales promotions | "A direct inducement or incentive offered to customers to encourage a purchase." | Coupons, samples, discounts • short-term, cost-effective, suited to stimulating a demand trough |
| Publicity | "Any free news story about a business." | Uses media as promotion at zero direct advertising cost |
| Public relations | "Creating favourable relationships with customers and stakeholders." | Press releases, community engagement, sponsorship, and crisis management |
| Opinion leaders | "A person who influences others' desires" — an actor, athlete or expert | Shapes perception and drives brand awareness |
| Word of mouth | "Customers influencing each other during conversation." | Most trusted because perceived as unbiased; also the most damaging — one dissatisfied customer tells approximately 11 potential customers |
| Why a mix is used | — | Each element reaches a different audience, builds a different kind of credibility, and hedges the risk of any one channel failing |

### TABLE 3.2 — CONNECTIONS

| Connects to | Specific point | Nature of the connection |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Market research | Promotion is only targeted where research has identified customer preferences, attitudes, brand preferences and buying intentions; NPS is both research input and control KPI |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **product life cycle** | Introduction = awareness-building; growth = brand acceptance; maturity = brand loyalty and reminder advertising; renewal = leveraging existing brand equity |
| **PROCESS** | **Establishing market objectives** | Converts an abstract objective (rebuild brand and reputation) into measurable campaign targets |
| **PROCESS** | **Developing marketing strategies (stage 5)** | Promotion must be consistent with the positioning set at stage 4, or the message contradicts the brand |
| **PROCESS** | Implementation & monitoring — **financial forecast**, **sales analysis** | The forecast estimates promotional cost; sales analysis measures campaign return; revising the marketing strategy covers pulling a campaign that misfires |
| **INFLUENCE** | Customer choice — psychological | Advertising operates on perception, attitudes and self-image |
| **INFLUENCE** | Customer choice — sociocultural | Opinion leaders and word of mouth operate through reference groups |
| **INFLUENCE** | Consumer laws — deceptive and misleading advertising | Prohibited under the ACL: fine print, bait-and-switch, exaggerated claims, distorted before/after images, unsupported "9 out of 10" surveys, country-of-origin claims |
| **INFLUENCE** | Ethical — truth, accuracy and good taste | Good taste is highly subjective, requiring awareness of community sensitivities around race, gender and religion |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Increasing market share | Advertising drives awareness, which drives trial |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Maximising customer service | Relationship marketing is a service objective delivered through a promotional tool |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Promoting ethical & socially responsible behaviour | Publicity and PR are how CSR becomes a marketing asset rather than a cost |

### TABLE 3.3 — QANTAS

| # | Sub-point | What Qantas did | Figures |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Advertising (legacy campaign) | Ran and relaunched its national-identity campaign | **"I Still Call Australia Home" debuted 2004 at $10M**; relaunched 2022 with Kylie Minogue and Hugh Jackman — **domestic routes reached 110% of pre-COVID levels within two months** |
| 2 | Advertising (current) | Targets new geographic markets using Australian imagery | **"Australia in the Sky" (2024)** aimed at North America; **"Feels Like Home Again"** noted for authenticity and genuine emotional response |
| 3 | Relationship marketing | Runs the Frequent Flyer scheme as its core loyalty engine | **16.4M members (FY24)**, loyalty activity **+11%**, membership **+14%**, delivering **$511M underlying EBIT FY24** and **$255M in 1H FY25** |
| 4 | Sales promotions | Short-term discounting to stimulate demand in slow periods | **500,000+ fares discounted over a single 72-hour period**; economy from **$109 one-way**, **25+ routes under $150**; **50% off points seats from Oct 2023** |
| 5 | Publicity and public relations | Sponsorship, charity and goodwill gestures generating free coverage | **30-year Wallabies sponsorship**; **$37.7M raised with UNICEF's "Change for Good" over 21 years**; **45,000 free flights** after the Rex and Bonza collapses (2024) |
| 6 | Opinion leaders and word of mouth | Uses a globally recognised Australian figure plus owned social channels | **Hugh Jackman** as global ambassador; **1M Instagram followers**; **8.5M monthly website visits**; **domestic NPS +24 pts (FY24)**, **global NPS +22 pts (2024)** |

**Net effect:** promotion measurably moved demand and loyalty, but did not offset the ghost-flights damage — customer trust still fell from 74% (Aug 2023) to 47% (June 2024).

## 4.0 — FACTORS INFLUENCING CUSTOMER CHOICE

### TABLE 4.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition | The specific factors |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Customer choice | "The decisions and actions of customers when they search for and purchase goods and services." | Allows a business to predict customer trends and maximise the effectiveness of its strategies |
| **Psychological** (intrinsic) | "Personal characteristics of an individual that influence their behaviour." | **Perception** (the opinion held about a good or service) • **Motives** (the reason for purchasing — safety, comfort, sustenance) • **Attitudes and beliefs** (formed from experience, family, education) • **Personality and self-image** (people buy what reflects them) • **Learning** (information from past experience) |
| **Sociocultural** (extrinsic) | "Forces exerted by other people and society." | **Social class** (occupation, income, education — higher class = higher discretionary income) • **Culture and subculture** (shared values, beliefs, traditions) • **Family and roles** • **Reference/peer groups** (primary = friends, frequent contact; secondary = celebrities, occasional) |
| **Economic** | Factors affecting willingness and ability to spend | **Upturn/boom**: rising income, low unemployment, high confidence, high spending — interest rates raised to slow inflation • **Trough**: high unemployment, low spending — rates dropped to facilitate growth • Has a more direct and immediate impact than the other three |
| **Government** | "The ways government actions and policies shape consumer decisions." | **Economic policies** (counter-cyclical fiscal and monetary policy) • **Laws and regulation** (age restrictions, taxes, subsidies) • **Government campaigns** promoting or discouraging behaviour |

### TABLE 4.2 — CONNECTIONS

| Connects to | Specific point | Nature of the connection |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **SWOT** and **PLC** | Customer-choice factors populate the O and T quadrants (an economic upturn is an opportunity; a cost-of-living crisis is a threat) and determine a product's PLC position |
| **PROCESS** | **Market research** — all three steps | Determining information needs → collecting primary (survey, observation, experiment) and secondary data → analysing and interpreting to find patterns in buying behaviour |
| **PROCESS** | **Establishing market objectives** | Objectives must be attainable given the economic environment; a recession forces objectives to be rewritten downward |
| **PROCESS** | **Identifying target markets** | Psychographic and behavioural segments are psychological and sociocultural influences converted into market divisions |
| **PROCESS** | Monitoring — **sales analysis**, **market share analysis** | A shift in customer choice registers as a variance, triggering revising the marketing strategy |
| **STRATEGY** | Segmentation, differentiation, positioning | Psychographic and behavioural segmentation are the direct response |
| **STRATEGY** | Price | The direct response to economic influences — penetration and loss leaders in downturns, skimming in booms |
| **STRATEGY** | Promotion | The direct response to psychological and sociocultural influences — advertising to perception and self-image; opinion leaders and word of mouth to reference groups |
| **STRATEGY** | Products (branding, packaging) | Branding responds to perception and self-image; the total product concept (tangible and intangible benefits) exists because customers buy the intangible |

### TABLE 4.3 — QANTAS

| # | Factor | What happened | Figures |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Psychological — perception | Perception tracked operational fact once reliability improved | NPS **doubled**; on-time performance **74.1% in 2023 vs 67.1% in 2022**, reaching **80% in FY24 against 44% in 2022** |
| 2 | Psychological — attitudes and trust | Reputational collapse followed by partial recovery | Customer trust **fell from 74% (Aug 2023) to 47% (June 2024)**; **RepTrak recovered 12 points to 67/100 (FY24)**, below its **80/100** peak |
| 3 | Sociocultural — family and reference groups | Released reward capacity to meet family and leisure travel needs | **Classic Plus (April 2024, est. $120M)** released **20M more reward seats**; redemptions **+13%** |
| 4 | Sociocultural — family roles and the social calendar | Capacity and pricing follow school holidays | **4.7 million domestic aviation passengers in July 2022** |
| 5 | Economic | Air travel is discretionary expenditure, making Qantas acutely cyclical | RBA tightening to a **4.35% cash rate cut first-half profits 13%**; industry international passenger revenue **$610bn (2019) → $189bn (2020) → $648bn (2023)**; Qantas revenue **$17.7bn (2019) → $5.9bn (2021) → $21.94bn (FY24, +10.7%)** |
| 6 | Government | Border closures removed demand; later policy protected and redirected Qantas | **~$2.7bn in COVID assistance (incl. ~$900M JobKeeper)**; government **blocked Qatar Airways' extra flights (2023–24)**; net-zero-2050 commitment set targets of **10% SAF by 2030, 60% by 2050** |

**Net effect:** a 27-point collapse in trust was converted into a 24-point NPS recovery, but a single interest-rate cycle still cut profits 13% — economic influences are absorbed rather than answered.

## 5.0 — CONSUMER LAWS

### TABLE 5.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition | The specifics |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Australian Consumer Law | "Introduced to protect consumers and provide uniformity of legal protection among all states." | Sits within the **Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth)** • two purposes: protect consumers against undesirable practices, and regulate practices that restrict competition • enforced by the **ACCC** • replaced the *Trade Practices Act 1974* |
| Deceptive and misleading advertising | "Creating a false impression to intentionally influence and deceive customers." | **Fine print** • **bait-and-switch** • distorted **before/after images** • unsupported **tests/surveys** ("9 out of 10 people") • **country of origin** ("made in" vs "product of") • **exaggerated claims** • **vague statements** • false **special offers** • illegal, though grey areas and loopholes remain |
| Price discrimination | "Setting different prices for the same product in separate markets." | Mostly legal — quantity discounts and age-based discounts are permitted • illegal where discriminatory or where it substantially lessens competition |
| Implied conditions | "The unspoken and unwritten terms of a contract of sale." | Products must match description, be **fit for purpose**, be **safe to use** and of **acceptable quality** (free from defects, durable) |
| Warranties | "A written guarantee that faulty products will be repaired or replaced within a set period." | Statutory warranties apply regardless of any manufacturer's warranty • a "no refund" sign has no legal effect • warranties both protect consumers and function as a marketing tool |
| Cost/benefit | — | Compliance imposes cost; non-compliance imposes penalty plus reputational damage, and reputation is the most significant intangible asset a business holds |

### TABLE 5.2 — CONNECTIONS

| Connects to | Specific point | Nature of the connection |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **SWOT** | Legal exposure is a weakness; regulatory tightening is a threat — lawsuits and parliamentary inquiries sit in the W quadrant |
| **PROCESS** | **Establishing market objectives** | Objectives must be relevant and lawful — an objective to maximise yield cannot be pursued through undisclosed charges |
| **PROCESS** | **Developing marketing strategies** | Constrains promotion (claims must be substantiated) and price (the displayed price must be the true price) |
| **PROCESS** | Implementation, monitoring & controlling | Compliance auditing sits inside monitoring; revising the marketing strategy is the mechanism for correcting an unlawful practice — refunds, re-displayed prices |
| **PROCESS** | **Market research** | Sugging (selling under the guise of research) is where consumer law reaches into the research stage |
| **STRATEGY** | Promotion | Advertising must be truthful and substantiated, constraining every element of the promotion mix |
| **STRATEGY** | Price | Pricing must not amount to illegal price discrimination, and displayed prices must be all-inclusive |
| **STRATEGY** | Products — branding and packaging | Packaging claims fall under deceptive and misleading conduct; warranties form part of the total product concept |
| **STRATEGY** | E-marketing | Online booking flows are where hidden charges and misleading availability claims arise |

### TABLE 5.3 — QANTAS

| # | Sub-point | What Qantas did | Figures |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Deceptive and misleading advertising | Sold tickets on flights it had already cancelled | **8,000+ cancelled flights still sold for up to 47 days**, affecting approximately **880,000 customers** |
| 2 | The penalty | Settled with the ACCC | **$100M civil penalty + $20M in refunds (ordered Oct 2024)**, compensating over **86,000** directly affected customers |
| 3 | Prior offence | Misled Jetstar customers about refund rights on delayed and cancelled flights | **Fined $2 million in 2019** |
| 4 | Price and disclosure | Changed how fares are displayed after ACCC action | **Now publishes the true cost of fares, including extra charges and levies previously hidden** from the displayed price |
| 5 | Fair competition / price fixing | Colluded with other airlines to fix fuel surcharges on freight services | **Fined $61 million (2007) and a further $20 million (2008)** — cartel conduct under the CCA |
| 6 | Compliance response | Built compliance infrastructure and improved warranties | Dedicated **Australian Consumer Law webpage** + **Qantas Group Audit and Risk policy audits**; **scrapped the 1 Jan 2024 COVID credit expiry in Aug 2023**; guarantees **$50–100/day baggage-delay compensation** and compensation for flights **over 6 hours late** |

**Net effect:** the response was reactive rather than preventative — the $120M settlement and the fall in trust from 74% to 47% exceeded the revenue the practice generated.

## 6.0 — ETHICAL

### TABLE 6.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition | The specifics |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Ethical behaviour in marketing | "Conducting promotional and advertising activities in an honest, transparent and moral manner." | Conduct that goes beyond the legal requirements — acting as a responsible corporate citizen |
| Truth and accuracy in advertising | "Presenting factual information about a product without deception or misleading." | Claims must be substantiated |
| Good taste in advertising | — | Highly subjective — requires awareness of community sensitivities around race, gender and religion |
| Products that may damage health | — | Health warnings required on potentially unsafe goods • restrictions on cigarettes, alcohol, gambling |
| Engaging in fair competition | The CCA requires businesses to compete fairly | Prohibited: **price fixing/collusion**, **cartel conduct**, **resale price maintenance**, **long-term loss leading** used to undercut smaller competitors |
| Sugging | "Selling under the guise of a survey." | Raises invasion of privacy and deception issues |
| Advantages | — | Builds customer trust • positive reputation • contributes to CSR • consumers who see a business as unethical stop buying from it |
| Disadvantages | — | Higher costs (sustainable materials, longer transparent advertising, ethically vetted suppliers) • can reduce short-term competitiveness • difficult to execute — an ethical claim is only as strong as the weakest supplier behind it |
| Why businesses act unethically | — | Where no law applies — e.g. **greenwashing**, or clinically ineffective products that remain legal to market • voluntary codes fill the gap: the **AANA Code of Ethics (2012)**, enforced by the Advertising Standards Bureau |

### TABLE 6.2 — CONNECTIONS

| Connects to | Specific point | Nature of the connection |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **SWOT** | Ethical reputation is a strength or weakness; sustainability initiatives are an opportunity; consumer backlash is a threat |
| **PROCESS** | **Market research** | Sugging is the ethical constraint on data collection — research must make its purpose clear and collect only relevant data |
| **PROCESS** | **Establishing market objectives** | "Promoting ethical and socially responsible behaviour" is itself one of the four marketing objectives |
| **PROCESS** | **Developing marketing strategies** | Constrains what claims may be made and which suppliers may be promoted |
| **PROCESS** | Monitoring & controlling | Reputation metrics (NPS, RepTrak, trust surveys) are the control KPIs; a reputational failure triggers revising the marketing strategy |
| **STRATEGY** | Promotion — publicity and public relations | CSR only converts into marketing value once communicated; PR is also the crisis-management tool when ethics fail |
| **STRATEGY** | Positioning | A values-based position ("Spirit of Australia", environmental leadership) is an ethical claim that must be continuously earned |
| **STRATEGY** | Price | Fair competition constrains loss leading and collusion; premium pricing during service failure is an ethical as well as commercial misjudgement |
| **STRATEGY** | Global marketing | Customisation must be culturally sensitive; standardisation risks giving offence in markets whose norms differ |

### TABLE 6.3 — QANTAS

| # | Sub-point | What Qantas did | Figures |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Environmental CSR | Committed to decarbonisation beyond legal requirement | **Net zero by 2050**, **25% emissions cut by 2030**, **$400M Climate Fund**, **$290M Airbus partnership** for an Australian SAF industry; **Jet Zero Australia + LanzaJet** plant targeting **up to 100M litres of SAF annually** |
| 2 | Environmental CSR — waste | Eliminated single-use plastics and lifted recycling | **200M+ single-use plastic items removed since 2019**, cutting landfill waste ~**75%**; **2,842 tonnes recycled in FY22-23** |
| 3 | Social CSR | Long-running charity and reconciliation programs | **UNICEF "Change for Good" raised $37.7M over 21 years** across 190+ countries; **Reconciliation Action Plan (2021)**; Great Barrier Reef and Katingan Mentaya forest conservation support |
| 4 | Ethical conduct in a crisis | Voluntary goodwill gesture beyond legal obligation | **45,000 free flights** to customers stranded by the **Rex and Bonza collapses (2024)** |
| 5 | Engaging in fair competition — failures | Collusion, ambush marketing and capacity dumping | **$61M (2007) + $20M (2008)** fuel-surcharge collusion fines; **ambush marketing at the 2000 Olympics** despite not being an official sponsor; ACCC accusation of **increasing seat capacity well beyond passenger demand** |
| 6 | Good taste and reputation | Marketing and pricing decisions that drew public criticism | Criticised for using a photograph of a **10-year-old Aboriginal girl living in "undesirable conditions"**; criticised for **charging high fares despite service failures and record profits** — **trust fell 74% → 47%** |

**Net effect:** a $400M Climate Fund and 45,000 goodwill flights sit alongside $81M in collusion fines and a 27-point trust collapse — ethical conduct that followed legal compliance rather than exceeding it.

## TABLE 7 — THE MARKETING PROCESS: EVERY STAGE AND SUB-POINT

*The marketing process is the series of steps a business undertakes to market its products to consumers effectively. It is completed in order.*

| Stage | Sub-point | Definition / detail | Qantas |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **1. Situational analysis** | **SWOT** | "A strategic tool evaluating an organisation's Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats." Internal (S/W — controllable) vs external (O/T — not) | **S**: 16.4M loyalty members, global brand, Oneworld + Emirates alliances, 26 countries, ~820 daily flights • **W**: fleet age 15 yrs vs Emirates' 10, thin balance sheet, union disputes, lawsuits • **O**: A321XLRs unlocking long-range routes, SAF, loyalty expansion • **T**: downturns, fuel prices, weak AUD, **Qatar Airways taking a 35% stake in Virgin** |
| | **Product life cycle** | Introduction (slow sales, low/negative profit) → Growth (rapid sales, rising profit, new entrants) → Maturity (sales plateau, saturation, intense competition) → Decline (harvest or divest) → Renewal | **Domestic and international are in maturity**; **Jetstar Group is in growth** • maturity response: fleet replacement, kiosks, moderating fares, brand rebuild • growth response: new routes, new aircraft, new Jetstar app and advertising |
| | Competitor analysis | Identifies industry trends and feeds the SWOT | **Virgin Australia** the main domestic rival (revived by Bain Capital); **35+ international competitors**, many government-owned with lower cost bases |
| **2. Market research** | Step 1 — determining information needs | What information would be useful | Customer preferences, attitudes, brand preferences, buying intentions, characteristics |
| | Step 2 — **primary data** | "Specially collected from original sources" — survey, observation, experiment | **In-flight surveys, mail surveys, complaint monitoring, discussions with customer contact staff** |
| | Step 2 — **secondary data** | "Previously collected for an alternate reason" — internal or external | **Government statistics, airline publications, interviews with competitor executives** |
| | Step 3 — data analysis | "Looking for relationships in raw data to develop meaning" | Identified customer "pain points" behind the **$230M customer investment (Sept 2023, FY23-24)** and improved food and beverage; **domestic NPS rose 110%+ by Dec 2023** |
| **3. Establishing market objectives** | SMART criteria | Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time-bound | See Table 8 |
| | The four syllabus objectives | Increasing **market share** • expanding the **product range** • maximising **customer service** • promoting **ethical and socially responsible behaviour** | Headline objective: **build two leading complementary airlines — Qantas (premium) and Jetstar (low fares)** |
| **4. Identifying target markets** | **Mass** approach | Targets a large, undifferentiated market | Jetstar for mass leisure travel |
| | **Market segmentation** approach | Targets a specific section sharing common needs | Behavioural segmentation by **trip purpose** |
| | **Niche** approach | A narrow segment of an already segmented market | Corporate/business travel — **~80% of the domestic corporate market** |
| | **Primary vs secondary market** | Primary = where most marketing resources go; secondary = smaller, less influential | Primary: corporate and premium • **>50% of capacity directed to Asia**, targeting **50% forecast regional aviation growth over 10 years** |
| **5. Developing marketing strategies** | The marketing mix — 4 P's + PPP | Emphasis varies with the objectives and the PLC stage | **Product**: Cityflyer • **Price**: yield adjustment (**international yield −6.6%, 1H FY25**) • **Promotion**: 50% off points seats (Oct 2023) • **Place**: route expansion + Qantas Distribution Platform |
| **6. Implementation** | **Developing a financial forecast** | "Estimating expected revenues, costs and profits" from projections of sales, expenses, production costs and pricing | Built from **statistical models, past sales data, executive judgement and consumer surveys**; also estimates the cost of market research, promotion, product development and distribution |
| **6. Monitoring** | **Sales analysis** | "Breaking down aggregate sales by product, segment and territory" | Identifies underperforming products for adjustment |
| | **Market share analysis** | "Comparing business sales with the performance of competitors" | Measured against the **65% combined domestic share objective** |
| | **Marketing profitability analysis** | "Measuring the profitability of products, segments and territories as a percentage of sales" | Behind the decision to move Bangkok and Gold Coast to Jetstar |
| **6. Controlling** | **Revising the marketing strategy** | "Taking corrective action where appropriate" — changes to the mix, new product development, product deletion | See Table 7B |

## TABLE 10 — REMAINING MARKETING STRATEGIES

| Strategy | Theory | Qantas (6 figures each) |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Place/distribution** | **Distribution channels**: direct (producer→consumer) vs indirect (via intermediary) • **Channel choice**: intensive (as many outlets as possible), selective (a moderate proportion), exclusive (one outlet in a large area) • **Physical distribution issues**: transport, warehousing, inventory | **97% of total sales are online** • **70% of domestic and 35% of international bookings** made online, **8.5M monthly website visits** • **Jetstar sells 90% of tickets online** • indirect via **Flight Centre and American Express**, with a **Computer Reservations System** supplied to agents • the **Qantas Distribution Platform** on IATA's NDC standards • owned retail through **Qantas Holidays, Viva, Jetset, Jetabout, TripADeal (bookings +60%)** • **+100 call centre agents across 98 locations** |
| **E-marketing** | "The practice of using the internet to perform marketing activities" — website, app, email, social media, SEO, PPC, content marketing, geo-targeting • **Advantages**: large reach, flexible, better data collection, inexpensive • **Disadvantages**: may mislead, security/privacy concerns, less control over criticism, technology-dependent | **8.5M website visits per month (2024)** • **1M Instagram followers** plus Facebook, LinkedIn, X, YouTube, TikTok • **monthly Frequent Flyer emails** with targeted deals • **tens of millions of the $230M customer investment** into the Qantas App • app launched with **baggage tracking (2023)**, cutting **mishandled bags 30% in FY24** • **240 self-serve kiosks** cutting check-in times up to **90%** |
