# How to memorise this essay

An analysis of the *Billy Elliot* Common Module shell, and the plan the app is built around.

## What you are actually holding

Fifty-five separate things, and they are not the same kind of thing:

| | Count |
|---|---|
| Quotes and scenes | 9 (7 spoken, 2 scenes) |
| Technique sets | 9 |
| Evaluation clauses | 18 |
| Turns and Closes | 6 |
| Shell and context sentences | 6 |
| Introduction and conclusion sentences | 7 |
| Red clauses to adapt, **not** memorise | 6 |

Treating all of it as one block of prose to be learnt word for word is the usual reason a shell essay collapses in the exam room. Different layers need different grip.

## Tier 1 — word for word, no licence

**The nine quotes and scenes.** A misquoted line stops functioning as evidence. Note that two of the nine are *scenes*, not dialogue — the dance of defiance and the final pit-cage cross-cut — so what you memorise there is the shot, not a sentence.

**The six Turns and Closes.** These are the only sentences in the essay that *assess* rather than describe, which is where the top band lives. They are also the shortest sentences you have to learn. Cheapest marks in the essay; learn them first within each paragraph once the evidence beneath them means something.

**The frame of each shell and context sentence** — everything around the red bracket.

## Tier 2 — exact terms, flexible order

**The nine technique sets.** Marks come from *naming* the technique, not describing the effect. The precise term or nothing: "axial jump cuts", "non-diegetic", "tricolon", "shot/reverse shot", "diegetic". The order you list them in doesn't matter; the words do.

**The eighteen evaluation clauses.** These are clauses, not sentences — the doc says to join two of them with *so*, *and* or a colon on the day and attach them to the quote. So the phrasing has to bend. Keep the idea and the verb; let the grammar move.

## Tier 3 — do not memorise

**The six red clauses.** A red clause you have memorised is a question you have ignored. Rehearse *rewriting* them against real question stems instead. The app's **Adapt** mode does this and saves your versions.

## Four traps specific to this essay

These come from what actually repeats inside your own nine pieces of evidence, and each one is a mark lost if you weld the right technique to the wrong shot.

1. **Shot/reverse shot appears twice.** Body 1 (Jackie's "Lads do football…") and Body 2 (Billy's "Just because I like ballet…"). Different jobs: Body 1's cutting *denies father and son a shared frame*; Body 2's *puts the cost on Michael's face*. Learn them as a contrast pair.
2. **The cage appears three times.** The gym cage in Body 1 (Jackie behind the wire — the enforcer imprisoned), the gym cage again in Body 2 (the dance of defiance shot through it), the pit cage in Body 3 (descending as Billy leaves). This is the spine image of the essay. Memorise the trio together and one image carries three paragraphs.
3. **Diegetic or not.** Body 2's crescendo is *non-diegetic*. Body 3's crowd cacophony and Swan Lake score are *diegetic*. One wrong prefix tells the marker you learnt a word rather than a shot.
4. **High angle down, low angle up.** Body 3 runs both — the high angle diminishes Jackie at the pit gates, the low angle elevates Billy in the leap. Daldry paired them deliberately; learn them paired.

## The spine

Every body paragraph runs the same six beats:

> shell claim → context (Daldry and the 1980s) → **three times**: quote → technique → two evaluations → **Turn** → **Close**

Learn the shape once and you only ever have to recall the fillings.

- **All three Turns open with "Yet"**, and each names its own cost: Body 1 **defeat**, Body 2 **purity**, Body 3 **purchase**.
- **Two of the three Closes open "Daldry positions audiences to…"** — recognise, then value. Body 3 buries the phrase mid-sentence, which is the one that catches people out.
- **Jackie carries three of the nine pieces of evidence**, in order: enforcer ("Not friggin' ballet") → defeated ("I never made it past Durham") → faith-breaker ("Give the boy a chance"). If you lose your thread on the day, walk Jackie's arc and the evidence comes back with it.

## The order to learn it in

1. **Introduction first.** Its three green phrases *are* the three body paragraphs. Learning it first gives you a hook to hang everything else on.
2. **Quote → technique → evaluation**, one card at a time. The quote is the retrieval cue for the other two, so it has to be solid before they go on top of it.
3. **Turn and Close last within each paragraph**, once the evidence beneath them means something.
4. **Then stop working paragraph by paragraph.** Mixing them up feels worse and works better — that mixing is exactly what stops Body 2's techniques leaking into Body 1.

## What a session looks like

Sessions beat hours. Four twenty-minute sessions across two days beat one long night, and sleeping between them is doing real work rather than losing time.

**If you have three days** — roughly three short sessions a day:

- **Day 1** — meet every line. Read a paragraph in **Read**, say it aloud, then **Coach** until the app stops offering new lines. Aim to have met the Introduction and Body 1 by the end of the day.
- **Day 2** — **Coach** first thing (yesterday's lines are due), then **Sort** to separate the confusable techniques, then **Blurt** Body 1 and Body 2 from cues alone.
- **Day 3** — **First letters** on every Turn, Close and shell sentence, then one full **Exam** under time, then **Weak lines** to mop up whatever the exam exposed.

**If you have a week**, the same ladder with more spacing: one Coach session morning and evening, Sort and Blurt from day 3, an Exam on days 5 and 7.

Two rules hold either way:

- **Say every new line out loud at least once.** Spoken lines are remembered better than read ones, and it costs nothing.
- **A line only counts as learnt after three correct recalls spread across separate sittings** — not three in a row tonight. That is the one number in the app worth trusting.

## Where the research comes from

- Recall to a criterion across spaced sessions — Rawson & Dunlosky, *Successive Relearning: An Underexplored but Potent Technique*, **Current Directions in Psychological Science** (2022).
- Testing beats rereading on delayed tests — Roediger & Karpicke, *Test-Enhanced Learning*, **Psychological Science** (2006).
- Spaced gaps beat massed study — Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler, **Psychological Science** (2008).
- Read–recite–review beats rereading — McDaniel, Howard & Einstein, **Psychological Science** (2009).
- Failed retrieval still helps if feedback follows — Kornell, Hays & Bjork, **JEP:LMC** (2009).
- Saying it aloud beats reading it silently — the production effect (MacLeod and colleagues).
