## 1.0 — MARKET SEGMENTATION, PRODUCT/SERVICE DIFFERENTIATION AND POSITIONING

### TABLE 1.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition (memorise word-for-word) | Build (say this straight after) |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Market segmentation | "Market segmentation occurs when the total market is subdivided into groups of people who share one or more common characteristics." | Aims to increase sales by better understanding and responding to the desires of different target groups • reduces resource waste and the risk of profit loss |
| — Demographic | Dividing by features of the population | Age, sex, income, occupation, cultural background |
| — Geographic | Dividing by geographic location | Urban/rural, region, climate, city size |
| — Psychographic | Dividing by personal characteristics | Attitudes, values, beliefs, lifestyle, motives, opinions |
| — Behavioural | Dividing by the customer's relationship to the product | Loyalty, usage rate, purchase occasion — *what they do*, not who they are |
| Product/service differentiation | "The process of developing and promoting products that are distinguishable from competitors' offerings." | Purpose is a **competitive advantage** • points of difference: customer service, quality, convenience, value for money, environmental and social concerns • gives the business more **leverage over price** |
| Positioning | "The development of a product image and perception in the mind of the customer relative to competitors." | The business must use elements of the marketing mix to shape and maintain the image • **positioning sets the foundation for all other marketing strategies** — which is why 1→2→3 is a *sequence* |
| Types of positioning | Quality positioning • Price positioning • Competitive positioning • Target-market positioning | Quality = superior features; price = affordability; competitive = countering a rival; target-market = focusing on the most profitable customers |

| Link to | Specific syllabus point | The link |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **SWOT** | Segmentation is only possible once a SWOT identifies which segments the business has the internal **strength** to serve |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **product life cycle** | Maturity demands segmentation and differentiation to defend share; growth demands expanded distribution |
| **PROCESS** | Market research — **primary and secondary data** | Segmentation *is* market research applied: surveys, observation and complaint monitoring are what define the segments |
| **PROCESS** | **Identifying target markets (stage 4)** | This is the stage segmentation directly executes — it decides mass vs segmentation vs niche, and which is the **primary** vs **secondary** market |
| **PROCESS** | Monitoring & controlling — **market share analysis** | The KPI that tests whether segmentation worked; a lost segment triggers **revising the marketing strategy** |
| **INFLUENCE** | Factors influencing customer choice — psychological & sociocultural | Psychographic and behavioural segmentation exist *because* perception, motives, self-image, social class, family roles and reference groups fragment a market |
| **INFLUENCE** | Factors influencing customer choice — economic | A downturn pushes consumers into the price-sensitive segment, so a business needs a brand positioned there ready to catch them |
| **INFLUENCE** | Ethical / consumer laws | Segmentation must not slide into discriminatory targeting or illegal **price discrimination** |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Increasing market share | A dual-brand structure defends the top and bottom of the market simultaneously |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Expanding the product range/mix | Differentiation is what justifies each new line |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Maximising customer service | Segments let service be tailored rather than averaged |

| # | Sub-point applied | What Qantas did | The stat |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Competitive positioning | Launched Jetstar as a no-frills counter-brand after losing the budget segment to Virgin | Domestic share **collapsed from 80% to 65% after Virgin entered in 2000**; **Jetstar launched May 2004** — the best cause-and-effect stat in the topic |
| 2 | Behavioural segmentation (dual brand) | Splits by trip purpose: Jetstar = cost-sensitive leisure, Qantas = premium/corporate, QantasLink = regional | Group now carries **60% of Australia's domestic passengers (H1 FY25)**; Jetstar domestic capacity **+8% FY24** (+15% group), leisure earnings **+54% YoY** |

| 5 | Target-market positioning | Prioritises the lucrative corporate segment via Cityflyer, lounges, Qantas Club and FFS | Holds roughly **80% of the domestic corporate aviation market**; **Cityflyer** (2001) runs Melbourne–Sydney every 30 min; **Sydney–Melbourne is the world's most lucrative route — $1.9bn in H1 FY23** |
| 6 | Quality positioning (external validation) | Positions as Australia's flagship carrier on safety and reliability | **3.2% cancellation rate in 2023 vs a 3.7% industry average**; ranked **#2 safest airline in the world (AirlineRatings, 2025)** |

**Judgement:** *Qantas has been astoundingly effective — segmentation did not merely defend market share, it recovered a 15-point share loss and converted it into the most profitable route in global aviation.*

## 2.0 — PRICE (PRICING METHODS AND PRICING STRATEGIES)

### TABLE 2.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition (memorise) | Build |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Price | "The amount of money a customer is prepared to offer in exchange for a product." | The **only** element of the mix that generates revenue — the other three cost money |
| **Method:** cost-based | Cost + mark-up determines the price | Cost + (mark-up % × cost) • guarantees costs are covered and a margin is made on each sale |
| **Method:** market-based | Price set according to the level of demand and supply | Higher demand or higher perceived value = higher price • requires extensive market research |
| **Method:** competition-based | Price set against what competitors charge | Below = undercutting (volume up, margin down, quality perception risk) • equal = matching • above = luxury positioning |
| **Strategy:** price skimming | "Charging the highest possible price for a product with unique features, lowered over time." | Maximises revenue early while the product is unique; price falls as competitors catch up |
| **Strategy:** price penetration | "Charging the lowest possible price to capture maximum market share." | Stimulates unsatisfied demand and switches price-sensitive customers from rivals; price can rise once loyalty is established |
| **Strategy:** loss leaders | "Products priced below cost to attract customers." | Bets the customer buys other full-margin products alongside it |
| **Strategy:** price points | "Setting different prices for similar products (a ladder)." | Widens the customer base across different perceptions of value and income • also signals brand position |
| Price and quality interaction | "The perceived relationship between a product's price and its quality in the mind of the consumer." | High price signals high quality and reinforces a premium position — **but only if the experience justifies it**; premium pricing during service failure destroys brand equity |

### TABLE 2.2 — THE LINKS

| Link to | Specific syllabus point | The link |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **product life cycle** | The PLC dictates the strategy: introduction suits skimming or penetration; maturity forces price competition and cost reduction; decline triggers harvesting |
| **PROCESS** | Market research | Market-based pricing is impossible without demand data; competition-based pricing needs **secondary research** on rival fares |
| **PROCESS** | **Establishing market objectives** | Price is the lever that most directly moves the revenue and market share objectives, and constrains the load-factor objective |
| **PROCESS** | Implementation — **developing a financial forecast** | Pricing decisions feed straight into the estimate of expected revenues, costs and profits |
| **PROCESS** | Monitoring — **sales analysis** and **marketing profitability analysis** | The two KPIs that test a pricing strategy; **revising the marketing strategy** is where fares actually move |
| **INFLUENCE** | Economic | Interest rates, inflation and cost-of-living determine willingness and ability to spend — and travel is **discretionary expenditure**, so price is the first lever pulled in a downturn |
| **INFLUENCE** | Ethical — engaging in fair competition | Competition-based pricing is legal, but **price fixing/collusion**, **cartel conduct**, **resale price maintenance** and **long-term loss leading** are illegal under the CCA 2010 |
| **INFLUENCE** | Consumer laws — price discrimination | Different prices for the same product in separate markets without justification; plus the duty to display the true, all-inclusive price |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Increasing market share | Penetration and loss leaders buy share directly |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Maximising customer service | Price and quality interaction — premium pricing funds the standard that justifies it |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Expanding the product range | Price points literally *create* the product range (Economy → Premium Economy → Business → First) |

### TABLE 2.3 — QANTAS EVIDENCE (6 stats)

| # | Sub-point applied | What Qantas did | The stat |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Price penetration | Uses Jetstar to hold the price-sensitive segment through a cost-of-living crisis | **11 million fares under $100 in FY24** (~10M in 2023, alongside 5M reward seats) |
| 2 | Price skimming | Charges a premium on unique, industry-exclusive ultra-long-haul routes | **20% premium on non-stop Perth–London**; Project Sunrise one-way **first class to New York est. $15,000** vs **$6,000 business, $1,500 economy** |
| 3 | Loss leaders | Priced below cost at Jetstar's launch to seize initial share | Fares **as low as $19 on Sydney–Melbourne**; **100,000 tickets at $49** plus a two-for-one offer |
| 4 | Price points | Four distinct classes across the ladder | **Sydney–LA business exceeds $8,000; first class reaches $20,000**, against ~**$2,500** for a comparable domestic premium fare |
| 5 | Competition-based pricing | Matches and undercuts new entrants route by route | Rex entered Brisbane–Melbourne: **Virgin matched at $79, Jetstar undercut at $65**; Bonza's 2023 entry drove Qantas' **average revenue per passenger 13.5% lower in 2023 than 2022** |
| 6 | Price and quality interaction | Backs premium prices with premium product — but must still follow the market | **$100M lounge refresh** and **Classic Plus (April 2024, ~$120M)** releasing **20M reward seats**, redemptions **+13%** — while **international yield fell 6.6% (1H FY25)** as capacity normalised |

**Judgement:** *Highly effective — the dual-brand structure lets Qantas occupy both ends of the price spectrum simultaneously, skimming $15,000 first-class suites while penetrating with 11 million sub-$100 fares, a range no single-brand competitor can replicate.*

## 3.0 — PROMOTION (THE PROMOTION MIX AND THE COMMUNICATION PROCESS)

### TABLE 3.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition (memorise) | Build |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Promotion | "The methods used to inform, persuade and remind a target market about a business' products." | Seeks to attract new customers, heighten awareness, increase sales and increase brand loyalty |
| Advertising | "A paid, non-personal message communicated through a mass medium." | Large reach and controllable, but expensive and one-way • TV, radio, billboards, print, digital, social |
| Personal selling | "The activities of a sales representative directed to a customer in an attempt to make a sale." | Tailored, builds trust, answers specific questions — but costly in wages |
| Relationship marketing | "The development of a long-term, cost-effective relationship with individual customers." | Loyalty programs convert one-off buyers into repeat revenue |
| Sales promotions | "A direct inducement or incentive offered to customers to encourage a purchase." | Coupons, samples, discounts • short-term, cost-effective, ideal for stimulating a demand trough |
| Publicity | "Any free news story about a business." | Leverages media as promotion at zero direct advertising cost |
| Public relations | "Creating favourable relationships with customers and stakeholders." | Press releases, community engagement, sponsorship — and **crisis management** when things go wrong |
| Opinion leaders | "A person who influences others' desires" — an actor, athlete or expert | Shapes perception and drives brand awareness |
| Word of mouth | "Customers influencing each other during conversation." | Most trusted because perceived as unbiased — and most dangerous: **one dissatisfied customer tells ~11 potential customers** |
| Why a *mix* | — | Each element reaches a different audience, builds a different kind of credibility, and hedges the risk of any one channel failing |

### TABLE 3.2 — THE LINKS

| Link to | Specific syllabus point | The link |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Market research | Promotion is only targeted if research has identified customer preferences, attitudes, brand preferences and buying intentions — **NPS** is both research input and control KPI |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **product life cycle** | Introduction = awareness-building; growth = brand acceptance; maturity = brand loyalty and reminder advertising; renewal = leveraging existing brand equity |
| **PROCESS** | **Establishing market objectives** | Converts an abstract objective ("rebuild brand and reputation") into measurable campaign targets |
| **PROCESS** | **Developing marketing strategies (stage 5)** | Promotion must be consistent with the positioning set at stage 4, or the message contradicts the brand |
| **PROCESS** | Implementation & monitoring — **financial forecast** and **sales analysis** | The forecast must estimate promotional cost; sales analysis measures campaign return; **revising the marketing strategy** covers pulling a campaign that misfires |
| **INFLUENCE** | Factors influencing customer choice — psychological | Advertising works on perception, attitudes and self-image |
| **INFLUENCE** | Factors influencing customer choice — sociocultural | Opinion leaders and word of mouth operate through **reference groups** |
| **INFLUENCE** | Consumer laws — deceptive and misleading advertising | Prohibited under the ACL: fine print, bait-and-switch, exaggerated claims, distorted before/after images, unsupported "9 out of 10" surveys, country-of-origin claims |
| **INFLUENCE** | Ethical — truth, accuracy and good taste | Good taste is highly subjective, so marketers must be alive to community sensitivities around race, gender and religion |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Increasing market share | Advertising drives awareness which drives trial |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Maximising customer service | Relationship marketing *is* a service objective delivered through a promotional tool |
| **OBJECTIVE** | Promoting ethical & socially responsible behaviour | Publicity and PR are how CSR becomes a marketing asset rather than a cost |

### TABLE 3.3 — QANTAS EVIDENCE (6 stats)

| # | Sub-point applied | What Qantas did | The stat |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Advertising (legacy campaign) | Ran and relaunched its emotive national-identity campaign | **"I Still Call Australia Home" debuted 2004 at $10M**; relaunched 2022 with Kylie Minogue and Hugh Jackman — **domestic routes hit 110% of pre-COVID levels within two months** |
| 2 | Advertising (current) | Targets new geographic markets using Australian imagery | **"Australia in the Sky" (2024)** aimed at North America; **"Feels Like Home Again"** praised for authenticity and genuine emotional response |
| 3 | Relationship marketing | Runs the Frequent Flyer scheme as its core loyalty engine | **16.4M members (FY24)**, loyalty activity **+11%**, membership **+14%**, delivering **$511M underlying EBIT FY24** and **$255M in 1H FY25 alone** |
| 4 | Sales promotions | Short-term discounting to spark demand in slow periods | **500,000+ fares discounted over a single 72-hour period**; economy from **$109 one-way**, **25+ routes under $150**; **50% off points seats from Oct 2023** |

## 4.0 — FACTORS INFLUENCING CUSTOMER CHOICE (PEGS)

### TABLE 4.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition (memorise) | The specific factors to name |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Customer choice | "The decisions and actions of customers when they search for and purchase goods and services." | Lets a business predict customer trends and maximise the effectiveness of its strategies |
| **Psychological** (intrinsic) | "Personal characteristics of an individual that influence their behaviour." | **Perception** (the opinion held about a G/S) • **Motives** (the reason for purchasing — safety, comfort, sustenance) • **Attitudes and beliefs** (overall feeling from experience, family, education) • **Personality and self-image** (people buy what reflects them) • **Learning** (information from past experience) |
| **Sociocultural** (extrinsic) | "Forces exerted by other people and society." | **Social class** (occupation, income, education — higher class = higher discretionary income) • **Culture and subculture** (shared values, beliefs, traditions) • **Family and roles** • **Reference/peer groups** (primary = friends, frequent; secondary = celebrities, occasional) |
| **Economic** | Factors affecting **willingness and ability to spend** | **Upturn/boom**: rising income, low unemployment, high confidence, high spending — interest rates raised to slow inflation • **Trough**: high unemployment, low spending — rates dropped to facilitate growth • **Have a more direct and immediate impact than the other three** |
| **Government** | "The ways government actions and policies shape consumer decisions." | **Economic policies** (counter-cyclical fiscal and monetary policy) • **Laws and regulation** (age restrictions, taxes, subsidies) • **Government campaigns** promoting or discouraging behaviour |

### TABLE 4.2 — THE LINKS

| Link to | Specific syllabus point | The link |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **SWOT** and **PLC** | Customer-choice factors populate the **O** and **T** (an economic upturn is an opportunity; a cost-of-living crisis is a threat) and determine a product's PLC position |
| **PROCESS** | **Market research** — all three steps | This influence is *the reason market research exists*: determining information needs → collecting primary (survey, observation, experiment) and secondary data → analysing and interpreting to find patterns in buying behaviour |
| **PROCESS** | **Establishing market objectives** | Objectives must be **attainable** given the economic environment; a recession forces objectives to be rewritten downward |
| **PROCESS** | **Identifying target markets** | Psychographic and behavioural segments *are* psychological and sociocultural influences converted into market divisions |
| **PROCESS** | Monitoring — **sales analysis** and **market share analysis** | A shift in customer choice shows up as a variance, triggering **revising the marketing strategy** |
| **STRATEGY** | Segmentation, differentiation, positioning | Psychographic and behavioural segmentation are the direct response |
| **STRATEGY** | Price | The direct response to economic influences — penetration and loss leaders in downturns, skimming in booms |
| **STRATEGY** | Promotion | The direct response to psychological and sociocultural influences — advertising to perception and self-image; opinion leaders and word of mouth to reference groups |
| **STRATEGY** | Products (branding and packaging) | Branding responds to perception and self-image; the **total product concept** (tangible *and* intangible benefits) exists because customers buy the intangible |

### TABLE 4.3 — QANTAS EVIDENCE (6 stats)

| # | Factor | What happened | The stat |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Psychological — perception | Perception tracked operational fact once reliability was fixed | NPS **doubled**; on-time performance **74.1% in 2023 vs 67.1% in 2022**, reaching **80% in FY24 against 44% in 2022** |
| 2 | Psychological — attitudes and trust | The counter-case: reputational collapse and partial recovery | Customer trust **fell from 74% (Aug 2023) to 47% (June 2024)**; **RepTrak recovered 12 points to 67/100 (FY24)** but remains below its **80/100** peak |
| 3 | Sociocultural — family and reference groups | Released reward capacity to meet diverse family and leisure travel needs | **Classic Plus (April 2024, est. $120M)** released **20M more reward seats**; redemptions **+13%** |
| 4 | Sociocultural — family roles and the social calendar | Capacity and pricing must follow school holidays | **4.7 million domestic aviation passengers in July 2022** |
| 5 | Economic | Air travel is **discretionary expenditure**, making Qantas acutely cyclical | RBA tightening to a **4.35% cash rate cut first-half profits 13%**; industry international passenger revenue **$610bn (2019) → $189bn (2020) → $648bn (2023)**; Qantas revenue **$17.7bn (2019) → $5.9bn (2021) → $21.94bn (FY24, +10.7%)** |
| 6 | Government | Border closures killed demand; later policy protected and redirected Qantas | **~$2.7bn in COVID assistance (incl. ~$900M JobKeeper)**; government **blocked Qatar Airways' extra flights (2023–24)**; net-zero-2050 commitment pushed Qantas to **10% SAF by 2030, 60% by 2050** |

## 5.0 — CONSUMER LAWS (WIPD)

### TABLE 5.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition (memorise) | Build / the specifics to name |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Australian Consumer Law | "Introduced to protect consumers and provide uniformity of legal protection among all states." | Sits within the **Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth)** • two purposes: protect consumers against undesirable practices, and regulate practices that restrict competition • enforced by the **ACCC** • replaced the *Trade Practices Act 1974* |
| Deceptive and misleading advertising | "Creating a false impression to intentionally influence and deceive customers." | **Fine print** • **bait-and-switch** • distorted **before/after images** • unsupported **tests/surveys** ("9 out of 10 people") • **country of origin** ("made in" vs "product of") • **exaggerated claims** • **vague statements** • false **special offers** • illegal, but "grey areas" and loopholes remain |
| Price discrimination | "Setting different prices for the same product in separate markets." | Mostly legal — **quantity discounts** and **age-based discounts** permitted • illegal where discriminatory or where it substantially lessens competition |
| Implied conditions | "The unspoken and unwritten terms of a contract of sale." | Products must **match description**, be **fit for purpose**, be **safe to use** and of **acceptable quality** (free from defects, durable) |
| Warranties | "A written guarantee that faulty products will be repaired or replaced within a set period." | Statutory warranties apply regardless of any manufacturer's warranty • a "no refund" sign is meaningless • warranties both protect consumers and act as a **powerful marketing tool** |
| The evaluation angle | — | Compliance costs money, but non-compliance costs **penalty + reputational damage** — and reputation is the most significant intangible asset a business owns |

### TABLE 5.2 — THE LINKS

| Link to | Specific syllabus point | The link |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **SWOT** | Legal exposure is a **weakness**; regulatory tightening is a **threat** — lawsuits and parliamentary inquiries belong in the W quadrant |
| **PROCESS** | **Establishing market objectives** | Objectives must be **relevant** and lawful — an objective to maximise yield cannot be pursued through undisclosed charges |
| **PROCESS** | **Developing marketing strategies** | Where consumer law bites hardest: it constrains **promotion** (claims must be substantiated) and **price** (the displayed price must be the true price) |
| **PROCESS** | Implementation, monitoring & controlling | Compliance auditing sits inside monitoring; **revising the marketing strategy** is the mechanism for correcting an unlawful practice — refunds, re-displayed prices |
| **PROCESS** | **Market research** | **Sugging** (selling under the guise of research) is where consumer law reaches into the research stage itself |
| **STRATEGY** | Promotion | Advertising must be truthful and substantiated — this constrains every element of the promotion mix |
| **STRATEGY** | Price | Pricing must not amount to illegal price discrimination, and displayed prices must be all-inclusive |
| **STRATEGY** | Products — branding and packaging | Packaging claims fall under deceptive and misleading conduct; warranties form part of the **total product concept** |
| **STRATEGY** | E-marketing | Online booking flows are exactly where hidden charges and misleading availability claims arise |

### TABLE 5.3 — QANTAS EVIDENCE (6 stats)

| # | Sub-point applied | What Qantas did | The stat |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Deceptive and misleading advertising | Sold tickets on flights it had already cancelled — the flagship case | **8,000+ cancelled flights still sold for up to 47 days**, affecting approximately **880,000 customers** |
| 2 | The penalty | Settled with the ACCC | **$100M civil penalty + $20M in refunds (ordered Oct 2024)**, compensating over **86,000** directly affected customers |
| 3 | A repeat offender, not a one-off | Misled Jetstar customers about refund rights on delayed/cancelled flights | **Fined $2 million in 2019** |
| 4 | Price and disclosure | Forced to change how fares are displayed after ACCC action | **Now publishes the true cost of fares, including extra charges and levies previously hidden** from the displayed price |
| 5 | Fair competition / price fixing | Colluded with other airlines to fix fuel surcharges on freight | **Fined $61 million (2007) and a further $20 million (2008)** — cartel conduct under the CCA |
| 6 | The compliance response | Built compliance infrastructure and improved warranties | Dedicated **Australian Consumer Law webpage** + **Qantas Group Audit and Risk policy audits**; **scrapped the 1 Jan 2024 COVID credit expiry in Aug 2023**; guarantees **$50–100/day baggage-delay compensation** and compensation for flights **over 6 hours late** |

**Judgement:** *Reactive rather than preventative, and therefore only partially effective — the $120M ghost-flights settlement and the trust collapse from 74% to 47% show the reputational cost of non-compliance vastly exceeded the revenue the practice generated.*

## 6.0 — ETHICAL INFLUENCES (TAPES)

### TABLE 6.1 — THEORY

| Sub-dot-point | Definition (memorise) | Build / the specifics to name |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Ethical behaviour in marketing | "Conducting promotional and advertising activities in an honest, transparent and moral manner." | **Conduct that goes beyond the legal requirements** — acting as a responsible corporate citizen |
| Truth and accuracy in advertising | "Presenting factual information about a product without deception or misleading." | Claims must be substantiated |
| Good taste in advertising | — | **Highly subjective** — marketers must stay aware of community sensitivities around race, gender and religion |
| Products that may damage health | — | Health warnings required on potentially unsafe goods • restrictions on cigarettes, alcohol, gambling |
| Engaging in fair competition | The CCA requires businesses to compete fairly | Prohibited: **price fixing/collusion**, **cartel conduct**, **resale price maintenance**, **long-term loss leading** to undercut smaller competitors |
| Sugging | "Selling under the guise of a survey." | Raises invasion of privacy and deception issues |
| **Advantages** (evaluation fodder) | — | Builds customer trust • positive reputation • contributes to CSR • *"if consumers see a business as unethical they won't buy from them"* |
| **Disadvantages** (evaluation fodder) | — | **Higher costs** (sustainable materials, longer transparent advertising, ethically vetted suppliers) • can reduce short-term competitiveness • **difficult** — an ethical claim is only as strong as the weakest supplier behind it |
| Why businesses act unethically | — | **In the absence of law** — e.g. **greenwashing**, or clinically ineffective products that remain legal to market • voluntary codes fill the gap: the **AANA Code of Ethics (2012)**, enforced by the Advertising Standards Bureau |

| Link to | Specific syllabus point | The link |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PROCESS** | Situational analysis — **SWOT** | Ethical reputation is a **strength** or **weakness**; sustainability initiatives are an **opportunity**; consumer backlash is a **threat** |
| **PROCESS** | **Market research** | **Sugging** is the ethical constraint on data collection — research must make its purpose clear and collect only relevant data |
| **PROCESS** | **Establishing market objectives** | **"Promoting ethical and socially responsible behaviour" is itself a marketing objective** — the tightest influence-to-process link available |
| **PROCESS** | **Developing marketing strategies** | Ethics constrains what claims may be made and which suppliers may be promoted |
| **PROCESS** | Monitoring & controlling | Reputation metrics (NPS, RepTrak, trust surveys) are the control KPIs; a reputational failure triggers **revising the marketing strategy** |
| **STRATEGY** | Promotion — publicity and public relations | CSR only converts into marketing value if it is communicated; PR is also the crisis-management tool when ethics fail |
| **STRATEGY** | Positioning | A values-based position ("Spirit of Australia", environmental leadership) is an ethical claim that must be continuously earned |
| **STRATEGY** | Price | Fair competition constrains loss leading and collusion; premium pricing during service failure is an ethical misjudgement, not just a commercial one |
| **STRATEGY** | Global marketing | Customisation must be culturally sensitive; standardisation risks giving offence in markets whose norms differ |

| # | Sub-point applied | What Qantas did | The stat |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Environmental CSR | Committed to decarbonisation well beyond legal requirement | **Net zero by 2050**, **25% emissions cut by 2030**, **$400M Climate Fund**, **$290M Airbus partnership** for an Australian SAF industry; **Jet Zero Australia + LanzaJet** plant targeting **up to 100M litres of SAF annually** |
| 2 | Environmental CSR — waste | Eliminated single-use plastics and lifted recycling | **200M+ single-use plastic items removed since 2019**, cutting landfill waste ~**75%**; **2,842 tonnes recycled in FY22-23** |
| 3 | Social CSR | Long-running charity and reconciliation programs | **UNICEF "Change for Good" raised $37.7M over 21 years** across 190+ countries; **Reconciliation Action Plan (2021)**; Great Barrier Reef and Katingan Mentaya forest conservation support |
| 4 | Ethical promotion in a crisis | Voluntary goodwill gesture beyond any legal obligation | **45,000 free flights** to customers stranded by the **Rex and Bonza collapses (2024)** |
| 5 | Engaging in fair competition — the failures | Collusion, ambush marketing and capacity dumping | **$61M (2007) + $20M (2008)** fuel-surcharge collusion fines; **ambush marketing at the 2000 Olympics** despite not being an official sponsor; ACCC accusation of **increasing seat capacity well beyond passenger demand** |
| 6 | Good taste and reputation | Marketing and pricing decisions that drew public criticism | Criticised for using a photograph of a **10-year-old Aboriginal girl living in "undesirable conditions"**; criticised for **charging high fares despite service failures and record profits** — **trust fell 74% → 47%** |
